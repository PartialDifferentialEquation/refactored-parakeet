01 The format

Build something that survives you

A weekend CTF ends before anyone has to live with their architecture. Censorfest runs for seven days in one venue against a defender who gets stronger every one of them — long enough that a clever hack written on Tuesday has to still be standing on Sunday, through three shift changes and two escalations, without its author touching it.

The infrastructure runs for 168 hours. The people don’t. Bridges accrue points at 1.5× while no member of their team is on the floor. The most profitable thing a Red Faction can do is engineer something that holds without them — and then leave it alone.

Six Red Factions of three build proxy bridges into the Range, a self-contained environment of fifteen services the organisers own. Twelve Blue Faction players hold the gateway every packet crosses, working three eight-hour watches around the clock. Ten organisers run the Range, arbitrate, and put the whole thing on air.

The scoreboard shows one number all week: aggregate Red bridge-hours, against the line the Blue Faction is trying to hold it under.

How Sudden Death works

At the top of every hour the Range publishes its next target set to the scoreboard. The catch: a rotated target is reachable only through a bridge that was already live before the rotation, and new bridges take twenty minutes to begin scoring.

You cannot rebuild your way through Day 7. The teams still standing at 13:00 are the ones that spent six days engineering for persistence instead of chasing captures — which is the entire thesis of the event, collected in one morning.

The Service Floor

The Blue Faction has to keep the Range usable while defending it. Synthetic probes run continuously against all fifteen services from a reference client; if availability drops below 95% in any rolling hour, Blue forfeits that day’s Service Floor award.

This is the rule that makes the defensive side a real job. Switching everything off is not a win, it is a forfeit — exactly as it would be for any defender who is not permitted to take the business offline to stop an intruder.

Disclosure Orders

Three times a day, the Blue Faction may compel one Red team to disclose the full configuration of one named bridge endpoint, truthfully, within ten minutes. A team may refuse once per event at a cost of 300 points, and the refusal is announced on the broadcast. False disclosure is disqualification.

It is the sharpest instrument in the game and it is not a technical one — which is the point. Infrastructure is rarely taken apart by packets alone.

05 The Range

Everything you attack is ours

The Range is fifteen web services built and hosted by the White Team: static content, authenticated apps, APIs, and one deliberately enormous file service that exists so that throttling matters. It is the whole of the target space, and it is the whole of the permitted space.

Nothing outside the Range is a target — not the public internet, not any third-party service, and not any real censorship system. The gateway enforces a destination allowlist, so out-of-scope traffic cannot leave even if someone tries: scope is a property of the network rather than of anyone’s good behaviour. Attempting to leave it is disqualifying, and it is logged. Techniques that would require abusing a third party’s infrastructure — domain fronting among them — are played against targets we host for the purpose.

Three networks

The Blue Faction is expected to break things. That is the job — which is why what they can break is kept strictly apart from what keeps the event running, and why no competitor machine has a route to anything but the game.

Every competitor packet routes through the Blue Faction’s gateway. Inspected, throttled, sinkholed and blocked in real time.

Production

Staff telemetry, internal chat and the broadcast encoder chain. No competitor device touches it at any point.

  • Staff and production only
  • Switch management lives here
  • Blue holds no switch credentials
  • No competitor-routable path

VLAN 30

Capture — one-way

Video only, and only outward. The broadcast path is not a network the competitor can use, and it is watched.

  • NDI|HX, ~15 Mbps per stream
  • Port ACLs: NDI to the broadcast host only
  • No gateway, no DNS, no station-to-station
  • IP forwarding disabled on the image
  • Alerts on any non-NDI traffic
  • Feature stations use hardware encoders

Competitor stations run a Linux golden image — every tool that matters here is Linux-native, from Wireshark and Zeek to every transport a Red Faction will reach for. Using the capture path to move game traffic is disqualifying, and it is instrumented.

07 Duty of care

Rest is part of the format

Not a concession bolted onto it. Every competitor is off-floor for eight hours in every twenty-four, and the scoring is built so that obeying that rule costs you nothing — your bridges earn more while you are asleep than while you are watching them.

Eight in twenty-four

Off-floor rest is required and badge-enforced. No scoring mechanic anywhere in the event rewards being awake at 04:00.

Medical cover

First aid cover with a named responder on duty at all times, plus a quiet room away from the floor and off camera.

Code of conduct

Published with applications and agreed at registration. Named contacts, and a reporting path that does not run through your own faction.

Your own room

Single occupancy by default. Sharing is opt-in with a named partner, confirmed with you before the event and never assigned blind.

On audits: inspections cover event-issued hardware and competitor desks only. They are announced, conducted by named White Team staff with the competitor present, and never extend to persons or personal belongings. In-game infrastructure disclosure is handled by Disclosure Orders, which is a scoring mechanic and not a search.

Do I have to stay awake for 168 hours?

No, and you are not allowed to. Every competitor is off-floor for eight hours in every twenty-four. The format is built so this costs you nothing: Red bridges score 1.5× while their team is away, and Blue watches hand over to a rested crew. The clock runs continuously; you do not.

What does “bridge-hours” actually mean?

Ten points for every hour a bridge is live, carrying real traffic, and undetected by the Blue Faction — multiplied by 1.5 for any hour your whole team is off the floor. A bridge that survives one night unattended is worth more than several captures. It is the closest thing this event has to a thesis.

What hardware do I bring?

None. Every competitor gets a standardised event laptop from a common Linux image with the toolchain pre-installed, so nobody wins on equipment. Personal devices stay off the game network entirely.

What exactly are the targets?

Fifteen web services built and hosted by the White Team, collectively the Range. Nothing on the wider internet is in scope, the gateway enforces an allowlist so out-of-scope traffic cannot leave, and directing tooling outside the Range is an immediate disqualification.

Is my screen really on the stream?

Any competitor screen can be cut to live, which is why capture is opt-in at registration, indicated on screen whenever it is running, and cuttable by you at the station. There is a 60-second delay with a producer kill switch. Log into event accounts only.

Can the Blue Faction just block everything?

It would lose them the week. The Service Floor requires the Range to stay above 95% availability in any rolling hour, measured continuously by synthetic probes; below that, Blue forfeits the day’s award. They have to defend the network without taking it away, which is the actual job.

Is accommodation included?

Yes — a room for the full week, three meals a day, and a 24-hour supply of coffee and snacks. Single occupancy by default; sharing is opt-in with a named partner and confirmed with you beforehand.

Will anyone search me or my belongings?

No. Inspections cover event-issued hardware and competitor desks only, are announced in advance, and happen with you present. In-game disclosure of infrastructure is handled by Disclosure Orders, which is a scoring mechanic you can refuse.
