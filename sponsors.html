01 What it is

Red team versus blue team, for a week

Censorfest is a 168-hour adversarial capture-the-flag event. Six teams of three build resilient network infrastructure; twelve defenders hold the gateway it all crosses, in three eight-hour watches around the clock. Everything is built on infrastructure the organisers own, and the whole thing is produced and streamed like a match.

The infrastructure runs for 168 hours. The people don’t. Attackers are scored on how long their work survives unattended, so the event rewards engineering that holds up overnight rather than heroics that photograph well. Rest is mandatory and rostered.

It is a training event

Both sides are doing recognisable professional work: transport engineering on one side, detection and incident response on the other, under a service-availability constraint that stops defenders simply switching things off.

It is a recruiting room

Thirty engineers, selected by application, observable for seven straight days under real pressure. You will learn more about how they work than any interview loop will tell you.

It is a broadcast

Continuous production with commentary, live dashboards and a full VOD archive afterwards. Your placement does not end when the week does.

Everything is played inside our own range. All fifteen target services are built and hosted by the organisers, the gateway enforces a destination allowlist so out-of-scope traffic cannot leave, and no third-party system is ever a target. Nothing in this event touches your infrastructure, your customers, or anyone else’s.

The handover is the format’s best asset

Three times a day the outgoing defensive watch briefs the incoming one on air: what they saw, what they blocked, what they think is still running out there. It is the most under-televised thing in the security industry and it is where the sponsorship value concentrates — twenty-one segments across the week, each one a natural, non-intrusive place for a partner to sit.

05 Staging

The pilot comes first

A 30,000-dollar title sponsorship in a first-run event with no footage and no audience history is a bad ask, and we are not going to make it. The flagship is preceded by a small pilot whose entire job is to make the flagship a real proposition.

First ≈ USD 16k

Censorfest Zero

Twelve competitors, 72 hours, locally recruited, no lodging. A compressed version of the full format on a single production rig.

  • Produces the footage the flagship pitch needs
  • Produces the first real audience numbers
  • Calibrates the Service Floor and the bridge-hour line
  • Founding partners are credited at the flagship

Then ≈ USD 134k

Censorfest I

The full 168-hour event described across this site: thirty competitors, three watches, continuous broadcast, fully hosted.

  • Runs roughly eight months after the pilot
  • Sold on evidence rather than projections
  • All nine slots available
  • Pilot partners get first refusal on their tier

Founding partner

Backing the pilot is the cheapest entry into this event that will ever exist, and it comes with first refusal on the equivalent flagship tier plus a founding credit that does not get sold again. If you want the Title slot at the flagship, the pilot is where that conversation starts.
